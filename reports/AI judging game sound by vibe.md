# Give the deaf agent rulers, not ears

An AI agent that cannot hear should judge sound effects mainly by **measuring them against numbers taken from the user's reference clips**. It should use listening models only to confirm what kind of sound it has, and the user only for a few loudness-matched A/B picks. The best audio-language model in the newest perception test scores **72% against 91% for humans**, and on timbre, the quality behind "warm" and "crunchy", the gap is wider still. GPT-4o-Audio sits near chance there ([SonicBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11039v1)). Measured descriptors do better. The Audio Commons timbral models track listener ratings at **r 0.86 to 0.93 for brightness, about 0.79 for warmth and 0.87 for hardness**, and they were trained mostly on non-musical, Freesound-style sounds ([Audio Commons D5.7](https://audiocommons.github.io/assets/files/AC-WP5-Surrey-D5.7%20Evaluation%20report%20on%20the%20second%20prototypes%20of%20the%20timbral%20characterisation%20tools.pdf)). Claude's API takes no raw audio at all ([SDK issue](https://github.com/anthropics/anthropic-sdk-python/actions/runs/22354407550)). So the "ears" have to be tools that Claude runs and reads. For Aeterna the working setup is mostly in place already: `tools/validate_audio.py` catches defects, earshot is installed, `tools/audio_ab.py` builds loudness-matched A/B files, and Godot Movie Maker records the game. The missing pieces are a **reference-to-target-numbers step** (timbral_models plus pyloudnorm), an optional cheap **Gemini description** as a second opinion, and a rule that the user only judges the final two or three candidates for each sound. The method fails if the agent tunes a sound until one model's score looks good, if it trusts scores on very short hits, or if it judges a WAV instead of what the game actually plays.

## Audio AI recognises sounds well and judges timbre poorly

The newest direct test of what audio models perceive is SonicBench (January 2026). It covers 36 systems on 12 physical attributes: brightness, loudness, timbre, texture, reverberation and more. **Humans scored 91% overall, the best model (Qwen3-Omni) 72%, and about half the models sat near the 50% chance line** ([SonicBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11039v1)). Timbre is the dimension that matters most for vibe words. There humans reach about 0.97, Qwen3-Omni about 0.73, Kimi-Audio about 0.59 and **GPT-4o-Audio about 0.46**, yet almost every model recognises texture well (0.92 to 0.99) ([SonicBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11039v1)). Two more findings shape how a model should be asked. First, models are **worse at "which of A and B is brighter?" than at describing one clip**, which is the reverse of humans. Second, simple probes on the models' frozen audio encoders often beat the full language models, so the information is in the audio features and gets lost when the model puts it into words ([SonicBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11039v1)). In practice: ask a model to describe or score one clip at a time, never to choose between two, and check its words against measured numbers.

Running the strongest open model locally is not realistic on this laptop. Qwen3-Omni's audio Captioner is Apache-2.0 but has 30B parameters; a third party estimates **about 19 GB of graphics memory even when compressed** ([cloudprice.net](https://cloudprice.net/models/alibaba-qwen3-omni-30b-a3b-captioner); [HF card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Captioner)). NVIDIA's Audio Flamingo 3 is **non-commercial and Linux-only**, which rules it out for a shipped game ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/audio-flamingo-3-chat)). The cheap cloud route is Gemini. Flash-Lite tiers charge **$0.30 per million audio input tokens at 25 tokens per second of audio**, and a free tier exists ([Gemini pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). By my arithmetic a 3-second effect is about 75 tokens, so hundreds of descriptions cost cents. Another Google page says 32 tokens per second, which barely changes the cost ([Gemini token docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/tokens?lang=go)). OpenAI's gpt-audio costs **$32 per million audio tokens** ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-audio-1.5)), about 100 times more, and its predecessor did worst on timbre, so it is the weakest pick.

The text-and-audio embedding models known as CLAP sit between "listening model" and "measurement". LAION-CLAP (CC0, `pip install laion-clap`) lines up best with human timbre words like brightness, roughness and warmth among four models tested, but **only partly. EQ changes such as "muffled" or "bright" are encoded less reliably than reverb** ([arXiv 2510.14249](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14249)). That is exactly the kind of change Aeterna's pipeline makes. earshot, already installed for grain screening, wraps CLAP and an AudioSet classifier on the CPU. Its author reports that a "strong" match is the right kind of sound **98% of the time**. The author also says it cannot judge taste or emotion, hears nothing above 14 kHz, and often returns "unsure" on one-shots shorter than about 0.5 s ([earshot README](https://github.com/Leonxlnx/earshot), [LIMITS.md](https://github.com/Leonxlnx/earshot/blob/main/docs/LIMITS.md)). So CLAP-style tools answer "is this still a wooden knock?" They do not answer "is it warm enough?"

## Validated timbre numbers beat model adjectives

Each vibe word needs a measurement behind it, and published listening tests show how far each measurement can be trusted. The Audio Commons `timbral_models` package (Apache-2.0, `pip install timbral_models`, pure Python) predicts eight timbre qualities. Seven are regressions trained on 0-100 listener ratings ([GitHub](https://github.com/AudioCommons/timbral_models)). Its weak spots are documented: **boominess was only r 0.67**, it was designed for in-car road noise, and only 6 listeners rated it ([D5.7](https://audiocommons.github.io/assets/files/AC-WP5-Surrey-D5.7%20Evaluation%20report%20on%20the%20second%20prototypes%20of%20the%20timbral%20characterisation%20tools.pdf)). Plain spectral centroid, the usual one-number "brightness", **predicts perceived brightness at only r 0.51** ([Schubert & Wolfe 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/SchubertWolfe06.pdf)). "Punch" has its own perceptual model (Fenton, r 0.85) that beats crest factor and peak-to-loudness ratio. It exists only as a VST plug-in, not in Python ([Huddersfield](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/evaluation-of-a-perceptually-based-model-of-punch-with-music-mate/)).

| Vibe word | What the agent measures | How far to trust it |
|---|---|---|
| bright / harsh | timbral brightness, sharpness, 2-8 kHz share | high (r 0.86-0.93; sharpness 0.78) |
| hard / soft attack | timbral hardness, attack time | high (r 0.87) |
| deep / small | timbral depth | high (r 0.89) |
| warm | timbral warmth, 100 Hz-1 kHz vs highs | medium (r 0.79) |
| rough / crunchy | timbral roughness, high-band flatness | low-medium (r 0.56-0.70) |
| boomy | boominess, under-200 Hz share | low (r 0.67) |
| punchy | attack time + crest factor (proxy) | low: the validated model is not in Python |
| muffled | spectral rolloff, tilt | direct measurement; do not trust CLAP for it |
| cozy, toy-like | no metric; a mix of the rows above | only through the user's references |

Correlations from [D5.7](https://audiocommons.github.io/assets/files/AC-WP5-Surrey-D5.7%20Evaluation%20report%20on%20the%20second%20prototypes%20of%20the%20timbral%20characterisation%20tools.pdf) and [Fenton](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/evaluation-of-a-perceptually-based-model-of-punch-with-music-mate/). The muffled and cozy rows are my inference.

Finding defects is a separate job and needs no AI. Clipping shows up as spikes at the edges of the amplitude histogram; one published detector reaches an F-measure of **0.92** with a histogram plus waveform shape ([Laguna & Lerch 2016](https://researchr.org/publication/lagunaefficient2016)). DC offset, start and end clicks, bit-crushing (too few distinct sample values) and a hard cut-off well below the top of the spectrum (a low-rate or lossy source) are simple checks on the signal. `tools/validate_audio.py` already does most of them. The learned "quality" scorers fit poorly. DNSMOS, NISQA and UTMOS were built for speech ([DNSMOS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.15258)). Meta's Audiobox Aesthetics is the one scorer trained on sound effects too, but on sound it **matches human ratings at only r 0.57-0.65** and was trained on clips of 10-30 s ([arXiv 2502.05139](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05139v1)). Use it, if at all, to compare versions of one sound, never as an approval. Fréchet Audio Distance, the standard "how close is our set to theirs" number, **shifts with the number of clips** ([Gui et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01616v2)). With 5 to 20 sounds it is not meaningful. Distance from each of our clips to the reference set is the honest replacement (my inference).

Loudness has one trap that matters for game effects. The standard meter (pyloudnorm, MIT) averages over **400 ms blocks by default** and lets you set a shorter block ([pyloudnorm](https://github.com/csteinmetz1/pyloudnorm)). Many of Aeterna's hits are shorter than one block. My inference: measure with a 100-200 ms block and also report peak and RMS, and test what the meter returns on a 150 ms clip before trusting it.

## A reference becomes a target band, not a single number

The key move is to turn "make it like this" into numbers before any tuning starts. A short effect gives a noisy spectrum, so the agent should measure **several reference clips of the same kind** (all placement thunks, all fire hits) and store each feature's **average and spread**. A candidate is then on target, too bright, or too dull by how many spreads it sits from the average. That is my synthesis of the advice to average across a category and compare by rank rather than by fixed thresholds ([LTAS limits, arXiv 2403.18636](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.18636)). Spectrum matching itself is standard DSP and fits Aeterna's numpy/scipy pipeline. Average both spectra, take the ratio, smooth it, cap the boost, and build a minimum-phase filter so transients do not ring early ([linux-audio-dev](https://lists.linuxaudio.org/hyperkitty/list/linux-audio-dev@lists.linuxaudio.org/2023/6); [DeepAFx-ST baseline](https://huggingface.co/spaces/mikeross/deepafx-st/blob/main/deepafx_st/models/baselines.py); [Voxengo](https://www.voxengo.com/files/userguides/VoxengoCurveEQ_en.pdf/getbyname/Voxengo%20CurveEQ%20User%20Guide%20en.pdf)). Matchering does the same for whole tracks: level, frequency response, peak and stereo width ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/matchering/2.0.1/)). All of this is ordinary processing of the project's own recorded grains, so no AI audio is created. One project-specific limit applies. Aeterna's direction rolls off everything above about 10 kHz (`docs/AUDIO_DIRECTION.md`), so the agent must match the reference only below that line, or it will "fix" a deliberate choice.

The "punch" and "softness" part of a vibe lives in the envelope more than in the spectrum. Brightness itself depends partly on attack speed, not only on frequency content ([Saitis et al.](https://comma.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/assets/pdf/s13414-024-02934-2.pdf)). The target set should therefore include attack time, decay, crest factor and transient-to-body ratio next to the timbral scores. This is my inference from the sources above.

The agent can also tune on its own, but within limits. FxSearcher searches settings of an ordinary effects chain (EQ, pitch, filter, reverb) to match a text prompt, scored by CLAP, and reports results that agree with human preference ([arXiv 2511.14138](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14138)). The same pattern, with timbral numbers in place of CLAP, lets the agent nudge a sound toward its target band. The catch is the one earshot's author states for his own tool: an optimiser "can be pleased without the sound getting better", and a model is easy to fool ([earshot LIMITS](https://github.com/Leonxlnx/earshot/blob/main/docs/LIMITS.md)). The safe rule is to tune against several independent measures together, and to stop when the sound lands inside the band rather than pushing it to the middle.

Where the references come from matters for the law and for the data. Analysis-only use falls under the EU's text-and-data-mining idea, which covers sound. The general exception needs lawful access and lets rightholders opt out ([Kluwer](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-new-copyright-directive-text-and-data-mining-articles-3-and-4/); [Bird & Bird](https://mediawrites.twobirds.com/post/102j3s6/the-eu-copyright-directive-new-exception-for-text-and-data-mining)). This is not legal advice, and YouTube's terms were not researched. The safe practice: the user records short clips themselves, they stay in git-ignored `_work/`, only the derived numbers are kept, and nothing is shipped. A gameplay video also mixes music and other sounds under the effect. I infer that the useful moments are exposed ones (a menu tick, a placement in a quiet scene), and the agent should reject clips whose spectrum shows music underneath. Freesound's API has a `similar` search in a LAION-CLAP space with a licence filter ([Freesound API](https://freesound.org/docs/api/resources_apiv2.html)). The team noted on 2026-11-08 that no API key is possible (`art/decisions.md`), so Freesound stays a browser source picked by hand.

## A few blind A/B picks can calibrate the rest

The user's ear is the scarcest resource, and the research on pairwise comparison says how to spend it. **Choosing pairs at random wastes most comparisons. Choosing them actively makes a "tremendous" difference** ([Jamieson & Nowak](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1109.3701)), and a repeated quicksort is a simple, robust strategy ([Maystre & Grossglauser](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/229163/files/robustsort.pdf?version=1)). The nearest audio cases are hearing-aid fitting. With adaptive pairwise comparisons, preferences were found in **28 comparisons in simulation** ([arXiv 2406.09634](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09634)), and with a **median of 25 in a study, half the best baseline** ([Univ. Iowa](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Personalizing-over-the-counter-hearing-aids-using-pairwise/9984258850902771)). Nobody has measured this for game effects. My inference is that if the agent narrows each sound slot to two or three finalists, a handful of picks per slot is enough. Each pick should also be recorded with the finalists' measured numbers, so later slots start from the user's learned taste. Example: "he keeps choosing the darker, slower-attack take".

Every comparison must be **loudness-matched**, because a louder sound tends to win. The controlled evidence is thin: the best is a 2025 study that found a level bias of about -8.9 dB when people reproduced street sounds ([Acta Acustica 2025](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/abs/2025/01/aacus250028/aacus250028.html)). `tools/audio_ab.py` already matches loudness. It should also randomise which file is A and which is B. The judging effort should be weighted toward the sound players hear most. Dorfromantik's designer put it as "the interaction the player does the most has to feel as satisfying as possible", while nothing may break the calm ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/sparking-joy-through-tile-placement-in-idyllic-village-builder-i-dorfromantik-i-)). For Aeterna that means tower placement and the pinch ticks. Game audio teams pin a vibe down with a handful of "audio pillars" plus an "audio target" reference ([A Sound Effect](https://asoundeffect.com/game-audio-design-document/)). That maps directly onto adjectives with measurements behind them, plus two to five reference clips per slot.

## The recommended pipeline for Aeterna

The agent decides in gates, from cheapest and most trustworthy to most expensive. (1) **Defects**: `validate_audio.py` must pass. (2) **Right kind of sound**: earshot or CLAP must call it the intended category. A short hit that comes back "unsure" is a non-answer, not a fail. (3) **In the reference band**: timbral and envelope numbers sit inside the per-category spread, below the 10 kHz roll-off. (4) **Optional second opinion**: Gemini describes the single clip in words, sent at full sample rate. One ready-made tool downsamples to 16 kHz first, which removes everything above 8 kHz ([music-perception-mcp](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/AnqiPinku/music-perception-mcp)). Where the words and the numbers disagree, the numbers win and the report says so. (5) **In-game check**: record the real game output with Movie Maker and measure it again. The recent fix that turned off Godot's 5 kHz 3D low-pass (commit `fbcd194`) shows a WAV can pass every check and still sound different in the game. (6) **Human**: only the last two or three, blind and loudness-matched.

| Rank | Do this | Why | Cost and risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Install `timbral_models` and `pyloudnorm` in a venv (pip, Apache-2.0 / MIT) and write `tools/audio_reference.py`: reference clips in, per-category average and spread out, candidates scored as "spreads from target" | The best-validated "vibe" numbers; all local | Free; timbral_models is unmaintained, so pin versions ([GitHub](https://github.com/AudioCommons/timbral_models)) |
| 2 | The user records 2-5 exposed clips per sound slot from games they like; they stay in `_work/refs/<slot>/` | Turns "like that" into targets | Analysis only, never shipped |
| 3 | Keep earshot for the category check and defect sheets; never optimise against it | Already installed; the author's limits are clear | Unsure on clips under 0.5 s |
| 4 | Movie Maker capture of each changed sound in the game, measured with the same script | Catches bus, filter and 3D-distance changes | Already possible |
| 5 | Blind, loudness-matched A/B of the finalists, picks logged with their numbers | Few judgements, and they teach the agent the user's taste | Needs the user's ear |
| 6 | Optional Gemini Flash-Lite key in `local.env` for single-clip descriptions | Cheap words as a cross-check | Uploads audio: needs the user's OK |
| 7 | Optional Audiobox Aesthetics, for comparing versions of one sound only | Coarse "got worse" alarm | r 0.57-0.65 on sound; untested on short clips |

Skip local Qwen3-Omni (graphics memory), Audio Flamingo 3 (non-commercial, Linux), Essentia (no Windows wheel, AGPL ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/essentia/))), FAD on small sets, and speech quality metrics.

For references, the user should pick games whose sound they already like and that match a slot. Clips should be short and exposed, ideally with the game's music turned off in its settings (my inference). Dorfromantik is the best-documented cozy example for placement feel ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/sparking-joy-through-tile-placement-in-idyllic-village-builder-i-dorfromantik-i-)). Animal Crossing's team describes treating effects as seriously as music ([Animal Crossing World](https://animalcrossingworld.com/2012/11/highlights-from-an-interview-with-the-developers-of-animal-crossing-new-leaf/), secondary source). Tiny Glade's tactile clicks and thuds are a fan claim only ([fan wiki](https://shapes.inc/fandom/tiny-glade/author), unverified). Words alone ("cozy, warm, punchy") work only after the agent has turned each word into a row of the table above, and the user should confirm that mapping once.

For each sound the agent reports: the result in one line; a table of its numbers against the reference band, with every out-of-band value named in plain words ("darker than all your references"); the earshot verdict; the Gemini description if used; the in-game measurement; the spectrogram sheet; and what it could not check. Its confidence is stated honestly. "Warm" and "bright" rest on validated numbers. "Punchy", "boomy" and "cozy" rest on proxies.

The known ways this goes wrong are: tuning until a model is pleased (Goodhart); trusting scores on hits under 0.5 s; asking a model to compare two clips; references with music underneath; matching references above the deliberate 10 kHz roll-off; louder-wins bias in A/B; and judging a WAV the game then filters. **What to do first is rank 1 plus rank 2 for the single most frequent sound, tower placement.** That tests the whole loop on the sound where the user's ear matters most.

## Conclusion

The question changes from "can an AI hear vibe?" to "can the user's vibe be written down as numbers?", and mostly it can. Brightness, hardness, depth and warmth have measures that agree with trained listeners better than any audio language model agrees with humans on timbre. The two vibe words this project uses most, "cozy" and "punchy", have no validated measure, so they can only be pinned down by the user's own references and picks. The agent's real job is less about judging sounds and more about rationing the user's attention: settle everything measurable, then bring the user only the choices that are matters of taste.

What remains unproven is whether any of these measures hold up on very short game hits. None of the sources tested clips under half a second. The first placement-sound pass therefore doubles as the experiment that shows whether the pipeline can be trusted for the remaining slots before the 2026-11-18 deadline.
